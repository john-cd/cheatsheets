    Incident Response and Postmortems¶

    Use a shared response process when an outage, security event, data problem, or other operational failure affects users or could grow into a significant impact. The goal during response is to limit harm, restore the service, and keep people informed. Afterward, record what happened and turn useful findings into owned, verifiable work.

    This page is a starting template, not an incident plan for a specific organization. Define severity thresholds, escalation contacts, communication channels, legal and regulatory duties, and postmortem triggers before an event. For suspected security or privacy incidents, involve the designated security, privacy, legal, and communications teams; preserve evidence and follow the organization's restricted-access procedures. This operational checklist does not replace those plans.

    Prepare before an incident¶

    • Define what user or business impact starts an incident and how responders assign severity. Severity labels differ; write impact-based criteria rather than relying on a number alone.
    • Publish an on-call path, escalation contacts, and a reliable incident channel. Keep essential runbooks and status tools available if the affected service or identity provider is unavailable.
    • Name the people who can declare an incident, approve risky mitigations, notify affected users, and engage security or legal response.
    • Write down the service's user-visible symptoms, dependencies, dashboards, safe mitigations, rollback steps, and recovery checks.
    • Decide which incidents require a postmortem, who owns follow-up, and where the write-up and actions will be tracked.
    • Practice the process with a tabletop or failure exercise. Update runbooks when the exercise exposes a missing contact, permission, or decision.

    Coordinate the response¶

    Assign explicit roles as the incident grows. One person can cover more than one role for a small event, but the current incident commander and operational decision maker must be clear to everyone.

    Role Responsibility
    Incident commander Owns the response state, sets priorities, delegates work, and decides when to escalate or resolve.
    Operations lead Coordinates diagnosis and mitigations. Keep production changes coordinated and record what changed and why.
    Communications lead Maintains the incident record and sends scheduled updates to responders, stakeholders, and affected users.
    Scribe or planning lead Records the timeline, tracks requests and action items, and prepares handoffs.

    These roles are adaptable; a small team can combine them. As an incident becomes longer or spans teams, split the roles so the operations lead can focus on safe mitigation while the commander coordinates and the communications lead handles updates. See Google's incident management guide for one example of role-based incident command.

    A response sequence¶

    1. Confirm the symptom and protect people. Check user-facing service indicators, reports, and recent changes. For safety, privacy, or security risks, use the organization's designated emergency path.
    2. Declare and scope. If the impact meets a pre-agreed trigger, declare an incident early. Record the start time, affected functions and regions, known user impact, severity, and what is still unknown. Reassess severity as evidence changes.
    3. Open a shared record. Start a durable incident document and channel. Put the current impact, incident commander, operations lead, next actions, and next update time at the top.
    4. Reduce impact. Prefer a safe, reversible mitigation that restores the user-visible service. State the hypothesis and expected effect before acting; record the change and check whether it helped. Preserve logs and evidence when security or data integrity may be involved.
    5. Communicate on a cadence. Tell affected people what is known, what is unknown, what they can do, and when the next update will arrive. Do not wait for a confirmed root cause to acknowledge an impact.
    6. Hand off explicitly. Transfer the impact summary, current state, open risks, recent changes, and next steps. The new commander should acknowledge the handoff in the shared channel before the prior commander leaves.
    7. Verify recovery. Check customer-facing indicators, error rates, queued work, data integrity, and dependent services. Keep monitoring through the agreed recovery window before resolving the incident.
    8. Close and follow up. Record the resolution time and remaining risks. Create tracked follow-up items and start a postmortem when the event meets the organization's criteria.

    Status update example¶

    This fictional example shows the level of detail for a brief update; replace the times and impact with evidence from the actual event:

    Status: Investigating
Impact: Some report-generation requests are timing out. No data loss is confirmed.
Started: 14:05 UTC, based on the first verified customer symptom.
Action: The operations lead paused the worker rollout while checking queue health.
Next update: 14:30 UTC, or sooner if impact changes.

    Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses. If a fact changes, correct the shared record rather than leaving contradictory updates in different channels.

    Write a useful postmortem¶

    A postmortem is a learning record, not a verdict about a person. Agree on triggers such as material user impact, data loss or exposure, emergency rollback, a prolonged recovery, or a monitoring failure before an incident occurs. Not every alert needs a full postmortem; the team should still record small events when a pattern or a missing safeguard is worth learning from.

    Write while evidence is available, using the review window defined by the organization. Focus on what the system and team knew at each point, the conditions that shaped decisions, and how safeguards performed. Blameless writing does not hide mistakes or remove accountability: it explains how interfaces, tools, alerts, procedures, staffing, and information affected the outcome, then assigns improvements that can be verified.

    Postmortem template¶

    # [Short incident title]

- Status: Draft / Reviewed
- Incident ID and severity: [link and impact-based level]
- Service and owner: [service, team, incident commander]
- Incident window: [start and end, including timezone]
- Authors and review date: [names or roles, date]

## Summary

[What users experienced, for how long, and how the service recovered.]

## Impact

[Affected users, functions, regions, data, SLOs, and known limits of the estimate.]

## Detection and response

[How the event was detected, how the response worked, and what mitigations changed.]

## Timeline

| Time (UTC) | Observation or action | Evidence |
| ---------- | --------------------- | -------- |

## Trigger and contributing conditions

[Known trigger, causal chain, system behavior, missing signals, and assumptions that shaped decisions. Record uncertainty instead of forcing a single root cause.]

## What helped / what hindered

[Tools, procedures, coordination, and communications that affected the outcome.]

## Follow-up actions

| Action and intended effect | Type | Owner | Priority | Tracking link | Due or review date | Verifiable completion |
| -------------------------- | ---- | ----- | -------- | ------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- |

## Open questions and risks

[What remains unknown, who owns it, and when it will be revisited.]

    Use a timeline with absolute timestamps and a timezone so responders can correlate alerts, deployments, customer reports, and mitigation actions. Cite the dashboard, log, ticket, or message behind important claims, and distinguish measured impact from estimates.

    Make actions verifiable¶

    Split large efforts into trackable actions. A useful action has an owner, a priority, a due or review date, a tracking link, and an observable completion condition. Include prevention, detection, mitigation, and response improvements when the incident shows gaps in more than one area. Review open actions in the normal team planning process; publishing a postmortem does not complete its actions.

    Illustrative proposal Type Verifiable end state
    Add a user-facing latency alert for the report-generation SLO. Detection The alert fires in a staging exercise when the agreed threshold is crossed and links to the response runbook.
    Add a documented worker-rollout pause and rollback step. Mitigation A responder can follow the step in a staging exercise without production access from the author.

    These are example proposals, not changes deployed to a real service. A team should approve and track its own actions, then record evidence when each one is completed.
